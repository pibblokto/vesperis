# Ideas: ruins, audio shards, the signal radar

Notes from a discussion on 2026-09-30. Nothing here is scheduled or designed; it is the
record of two ideas and the first thoughts on them.

## 1. Ruins with audio shards

### The idea

Ruins of vanished civilisations become more complex and interesting places, and inside
them you can find **audio shards**: recordings that are either a spoken monologue or a
piece of music. Everything is generated by code, and each planet has its own sound: a
language of its own and a musical tradition of its own.

On the ship you **decode** a shard. Music can be listened to and named. A monologue plays
as alien speech, with a decoded text of what is said. The text is generated without an
LLM (grammars, trees, templates), so it stays deterministic and offline like the rest of
the game.

Some desert planets are, underneath, **post-apocalyptic worlds**: still the desert type,
but with ruins, and their shards tell the story of how things led to the catastrophe.

### Why it fits

The guide loop is "see, name, remember". Shards give the explorer something to bring
back and a reason to return to the ship other than to leave; the cabin's screens get a
purpose (decoding). Ruins are already a landmark kind found on foot and named in the
guide, so shards, the log entry, the guide's statistics ("shards decoded") and the naming
of a piece slot straight in.

### Music (the easy half)

The audio synth already exists. A planet's tradition is a small seeded set of choices:

* a scale of 5 to 9 notes from a generated tuning (not necessarily 12-tone);
* a rhythmic cycle (7/8, 5/4, additive patterns) and a tempo with drift;
* two or three synthesised timbres and a rule for ornamentation.

A piece is a generated phrase structure over that (statement, variation, answer,
return). After two shards from the same world the listener recognises the tradition,
which is the effect wanted.

### Language (the fun half)

Each planet gets a phoneme inventory (which consonants and vowels exist, the syllable
shape such as CV or CVC, allowed clusters), a prosody (stress pattern, pitch contour,
speech rate) and a voice (formant-like synthesis: breathy, clicky, tonal). The monologue
is rendered from the decoded text: the alien words come from the phonemes, spoken with
the planet's prosody. Speech and decoded text come from the same seed, so they agree in
length and rhythm.

### Text without an LLM

Doable, and the constraint is a feature:

* **A story grammar.** A tree of templates (an elegy, a warning, a hymn, a work song, a
  record of a season) whose leaves draw on vocabulary tied to the world. The planet
  function already knows what the world is like: the climate, the oceans, the mountains,
  the second sun, the rings. A monologue on a tidally locked world speaks of the line
  between day and night; one under a red giant of the swollen sun. That grounding makes
  the text read as intentional rather than random.
* **Recurring named things.** A culture generates a few proper nouns (a god, a river, a
  founder) and the shards of that world reuse them, so the reader builds a picture across
  shards.
* **Short and fragmentary by design.** A shard is a damaged fragment, partly undecoded.
  Gaps and ellipses hide the seams a long text would show, and fragments read as more
  mysterious than complete paragraphs. A few sentences each; long generated prose falls
  apart.

### Post-apocalyptic desert worlds

A trait on desert-class worlds (like the existing canyon or salt-flat traits) that raises
the ruin density, adds a second ruin style (larger, structured, roads) and selects the
"warning" branches of the grammar: rising heat, the seas going, arguments nobody listened
to. The world's own data can carry the story: such a world could keep dry seabeds and old
river networks from the drainage system, so a text about "the sea that was here" points at
terrain you can walk to. The link between text and ground would be the strongest moment
of the feature.

### To decide before building

* How many shards per world and how they are found (a few per ruin, scattered, hinted by
  the rangefinder?). Scarcity matters: everywhere, they become noise.
* Whether every world with ruins had a culture, or only some. Rarer is better.
* Tone. The game is quiet and melancholic; mostly ordinary monologues (a harvest, a child,
  weather) with the occasional apocalyptic one will hit harder than constant doom.
* What decoding costs the player: instant, or a wait on the ship while the computer works.

### Size and order

Music and language synthesis are each a solid milestone. The story grammar needs the most
iteration, because generated text is only judged by reading a lot of it: prototype it
first as a harness mode that prints fifty shards for a world, and judge that before any
audio.

## 2. The signal radar

### The idea

In the Stardrifter you can switch to a **signal radar**, sweep it around and sometimes
catch a signal from a far-off system: a civilisation's transmission. Accepting the signal
sets the target. When you travel there you are guaranteed to find ruins, but the
civilisation is gone: only ruins and shards remain. The signal can come from a desert
world with ruins or a felisian world with ruins.

### Thoughts

* **It gives the galaxy a reason to travel far.** Today every destination is chosen by
  eye or by name; a signal is the first pull from beyond the local neighbourhood, and
  the melancholy of arriving too late is exactly the game's register (light and radio
  travel at a finite speed: the signal you hear is old, and the arrival makes that real).
* **It composes with the shards.** The radar is the entry point, the ruins the place, the
  shards the payoff; a signal could even be a shard heard from afar, so that on arrival you
  recognise the voice or the music.
* **Determinism.** Which systems carried a civilisation must be a property of the galaxy
  (hashed like everything else), not of the sweep; the radar only reveals it. Then two
  explorers sweeping the same sky hear the same signals, and a signal's system can be
  shared by coordinates.
* **The sweep as play.** A signal should be found, not handed over: a direction and a
  strength that sharpen as you turn, noise and drift, a lock that takes a few seconds of
  holding steady. Distance (in light years, hence the age of the signal) shown on lock.
* **Rarity and cost.** Signals should be rare (one within reach every few systems, not on
  every sweep) and far (many jumps), or the guarantee of ruins makes them a cheat sheet
  for the collector. The journey is the price.
* **The same trait underneath.** "Had a civilisation" becomes a world trait (desert or
  felisian) that the radar, the ruin density, the shards and the post-apocalyptic
  branches all read, so nothing has to be special-cased at arrival.

### Tone by world type (agreed 2026-09-30)

Felisian worlds are the living ones, so a dead civilisation there reads differently from a
desert: the world went on, the people did not. Their shards are the quieter, brighter
ones (a harvest, a child, weather, a song for a season); the desert worlds carry the
catastrophe. A traveller who hears both learns the difference without being told.

## 3. Further ideas in the same direction (2026-09-30, all liked)

* **Star charts as shards.** Some shards are a map, not a sound: the culture's own sky,
  drawn from their world with their constellations and names. Decoding it on the ship
  overlays their sky on yours, so you can find the star they named, and sometimes it
  points at another dead world. Cultures that knew each other become a chain to follow.
* **Artefacts that are instruments.** A shard's music was played on something. Finding
  the instrument in a ruin (a generated shape, a generated timbre) lets you play a few
  notes of the world's scale from the cabin. Small and tactile; the tradition becomes
  something you can touch.
* **Ruins that read the world.** A ruin's layout follows what the people had: a coastal
  culture leaves harbours on the old shoreline, a mountain one terraces and cairns on the
  ridges, a desert one cisterns and long walls against the wind. The terrain already knows
  all this, so the buildings come from the same data, and the shards can refer to places
  visible from the ruin.
* **Graves and names.** A ruin holds a few marked places, each with a generated name in
  the world's language. The guide records them like landmarks. Nothing else happens.
* **Signals that are not from civilisations.** The radar sometimes catches something
  else: a pulsar's rhythm, a comet outgassing, a moon's magnetosphere. Not every lock is
  a story, the real ones stand out, and the radar stays honest as an instrument.
* **A culture's calendar.** A shard is dated in the culture's own reckoning, derived from
  their world's day, year and moons. Decoded shards sort into a timeline on the ship; the
  desert worlds' timelines end.
* **Lending your names.** The guide export already carries names to a friend. Decoded
  shards travel the same way: a friend hears the music you found without flying there,
  credited in cyan like their names are.
* **The last one.** On any dead world one shard is different: the final recording.
  Finding it is not signposted; the log notes it and the timeline closes. An ending per
  world without a goal in the game.

First pair to build alongside the shards: ruins that read the world, and star charts.
Both come almost free from data the game already has, and both make the galaxy feel
connected rather than sampled.

## 4. Second batch (2026-09-30, kept)

* **Roads that still go somewhere.** A dead civilisation's roads survive as faint lines in
  the terrain (compacted ground, a different material), readable from the landing zoom.
  Following one on foot or by buggy leads between the ruins of a world: a network to
  trace rather than points to find.
* **A language you learn.** Decoding does not give the whole text at once. The first
  shard from a world decodes about 30%; each further shard raises the share as the ship's
  computer learns the language, and earlier shards re-decode with the gaps filling in.
  Ten shards in, you read the world. Deterministic and without an LLM: the grammar knows
  which words are common. This turns a pile of shards into progression without a goal
  and makes every additional shard on a world worth finding.
* **Cultures per world: none, one or two.** A world with ruins may have had one culture
  or, rarely, two, one on each continent, with their own languages and traditions, whose
  shards speak of the other. Their catastrophe may be shared or not.
* **Weathering by age.** Ruins on a wet world are rounded and overgrown, on a desert
  sandblasted and half-buried, on an ice world sealed in the glacier. The same generated
  building eroded by the world's own climate model, so the ruin tells you how long ago
  without a number.
